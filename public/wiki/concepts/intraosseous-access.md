# Intraosseous (IO) Access

## Summary
Intraosseous access is a rapid method of providing vascular access in emergency situations when peripheral intravenous (IV) access cannot be obtained quickly and the patient is unstable (e.g., shock).

## Indications
- **Emergency/Unstable Patient:** Hypovolemic shock, septic shock, cardiac arrest.
- **Failed IV Access:** Multiple failed attempts at peripheral IV insertion in a critically ill patient.

## Procedure & Sites
- **Preferred Site:** Proximal tibia (wide, flat surface; far from sternum for CPR).
- **Other Sites:** Humeral head, distal tibia.
- **Method:** Can be placed manually or using a powered driver.
- **Capability:** Allows for rapid delivery of fluids, medications, and blood products; can be used to obtain blood samples.

## Contraindications
- **Local Infection:** Cellulitis overlying the insertion site.
- **Mechanical Issues:** Fracture at the site or previous IO attempt in that extremity.
- **Bone Fragility:** Osteogenesis imperfecta.

## Comparison to Central Venous Access
- **Speed:** IO is faster to establish.
- **Skill:** Requires less specialized skill and practice than central line placement.
- **Safety:** Clinical trials suggest IO is safer and faster than central venous access in emergencies.

## Common Traps
- Choosing central access over IO in an unstable patient $\rightarrow$ IO is the priority for immediate fluid resuscitation.
- Using NG tubes for shock $\rightarrow$ Splanchnic circulation is constricted in shock, making enteral absorption too slow.
