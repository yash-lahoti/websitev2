# Post-MI Depression Management

## Summary
Major depressive disorder (MDD) occurs in up to 20% of patients after a myocardial infarction (MI). It is clinically significant because it can reduce adherence to post-MI medical treatments and increase overall cardiac mortality.

## Management
- **First-line Therapy:** Combination of psychotherapy and pharmacotherapy.
- **Preferred Medication:** **Sertraline** (SSRI).
  - **Why:** Low risk of adverse drug interactions with cardiac medications and generally well-tolerated without significant cardiac effects.

## Antidepressant Selection in Cardiovascular Disease

| Class | Preferred/Avoided | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| **SSRIs (Sertraline)** | Preferred | Low drug-drug interactions; cardiac-safe. |
| **SSRIs (Citalopram)** | Avoid | Dose-dependent **QT prolongation**. |
| **TCAs (e.g., Desipramine)** | Avoid | Inhibition of fast sodium channels $\rightarrow$ slow cardiac conduction. |
| **SNRIs (e.g., Venlafaxine)** | Avoid | Associated with **tachycardia** and increased blood pressure. |
| **Atypical (Mirtazapine)** | Less Ideal | Sedation and weight gain (poor for obese patients). |
| **MAOIs (Phenelzine)** | Avoid | High side-effect burden and food/drug interactions. |
| **Trazodone** | Not for MDD | Primarily for sleep; extreme sedation and orthostatic effects in elderly. |

## Common Traps
- Choosing Citalopram over Sertraline due to "all SSRIs are equal" logic $\rightarrow$ remember QT prolongation.
- Overlooking the impact of depression on cardiac mortality $\rightarrow$ it is a prognostic factor, not just a comorbid symptom.
