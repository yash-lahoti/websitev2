# Acute Agitation Management

## Summary
Management of acutely agitated patients who pose a safety risk to themselves or staff. The priority is rapid stabilization and safety through pharmacological and non-pharmacological means.

## Pharmacological Treatment Ladder
1. **Verbal De-escalation:** Always the first step if safe.
2. **Rapid Sedation:** When verbal methods fail.
   - **Benzodiazepines:** (e.g., **Lorazepam**). Preferred for rapid onset; can be given IM. Effective for agitation, alcohol withdrawal, and sedative-hypnotic withdrawal.
   - **Antipsychotics:** (e.g., **Haloperidol**, Olanzapine, Risperidone). Used alone or co-administered with benzodiazepines for more severe combativeness.

## Contraindications & Ineffective Agents
- **Mood Stabilizers:** (Lithium, Valproate, Carbamazepine). Ineffective for acute agitation due to slow onset and titration requirements.
- **Atypical Antipsychotics (Specific):** Clozapine is not for acute use (risk of agranulocytosis).
- **Sedating Antidepressants:** (e.g., Mirtazapine). Ineffective for acute psychosis/mania and may worsen symptoms.

## Common Traps
- Choosing a mood stabilizer for a manic patient's *acute* agitation $\rightarrow$ remember that mood stabilizers take days/weeks to work; use benzodiazepines/antipsychotics for the acute crisis.
- Overlooking the need for IM administration in combative patients.
