# Male Breast Cancer

## Summary
Breast cancer in men is rare but significantly increased in patients with an abnormal estrogen/androgen ratio.

## Risk Factors
- **Abnormal Estrogen/Androgen Ratio:**
	- **Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY):** Primary hypogonadism $\rightarrow$ increased gonadotropins $\rightarrow$ increased aromatase expression $\rightarrow$ increased conversion of testosterone to estradiol.
	- **Obesity:** Increased peripheral aromatization.
	- **Cirrhosis:** Decreased estrogen clearance.
- **Genetic:** Family history; BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations.

## Presentation
- **Mass:** Typically a firm, unilateral, subareolar mass.
- **Advanced Signs:** Skin or nipple dimpling, induration, ulceration.
- **Note:** Often detected at an advanced stage compared to female breast cancer.

## Workup & Management
- **Initial Step:** Breast imaging (e.g., mammography) to distinguish malignancy from benign gynecomastia.
	- **Gynecomastia:** Typically symmetric, centrally located around the nipple, indistinct margins.
	- **Malignancy:** Often eccentric (off-center), well-defined or spiculated margins, may contain calcifications.
- **Confirmation:** Biopsy (fine-needle aspiration or core-needle biopsy).
- **Pathology:** Invasive ductal carcinoma is the most common histologic type (usually hormone receptor positive).
