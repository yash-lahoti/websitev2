# Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC)

## Summary
A systemic process involving the widespread activation of coagulation, leading to both thrombus formation and consumption of platelets and coagulation factors, resulting in hemorrhage.

## Acute vs Chronic DIC

| Feature | Acute DIC | Chronic DIC |
|----------|------------|-------------|
| **Etiology** | Sepsis, Trauma, Obstetric complications | Malignancy (especially mucin-producing: pancreatic, gastric, ovarian) |
| **Mechanism** | Sustained procoagulant exposure (e.g., LPS) | Periodic release of tissue factor from tumor cells |
| **Coag Studies** | Prolonged PT/PTT | Often Normal (compensated) |
| **Platelets** | Low | Often Normal |
| **Fibrinogen** | Low | Often Normal |
| **D-dimer** | High | High |
| **Bleeding Risk** | Very High | Mildly increased (e.g., mucosal) |
| **Thrombosis Risk** | Mildly increased | Very High (e.g., DVT) |

## Presentation / Exam Clues
- **Acute:** Shock, purpura fulminans, massive bleeding.
- **Chronic:** Asymmetric leg swelling (DVT) + intermittent mucosal bleeding (gums, nose) + signs of malignancy (weight loss, epigastric pain).

## Workup / Management
- **Diagnosis:** High D-dimer, evidence of both thrombosis and hemorrhage.
- **Management:** Treat the underlying cause (e.g., treat the cancer or sepsis).

## Common Traps
- **The "Normal" Lab Trap:** In chronic DIC, labs (platelets, PT/PTT) are often normal because the bone marrow compensates for the low-grade consumption. Only the D-dimer remains consistently elevated.
- **Trousseau Sign:** Chronic DIC in cancer is often referred to as a paraneoplastic syndrome (Trousseau syndrome).
